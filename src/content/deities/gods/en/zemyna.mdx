## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

The name Žemyna derives from the Proto-Baltic word *\*žemē* (earth, soil), stemming from ancient Proto-Indo-European roots (cf. Latin *humus*, Greek *chamaí*). She personifies Earth as the sacred mother, giver, and nurturer of all life. Historical records document her cult among the earliest accounts of Baltic religion. In Martynas Mažvydas' 1547 *Catechism*, she is listed among the pagan deities to whom villagers offered grain and libations. In Jan Łasicki's 1582 treatise *De Diis Samagitarum* (*On the Gods of the Samogitians*), she appears as *Žemynėlė*, accompanied by ritual ale and bread offerings (*Žemyneliauti*).

Lithuanian mythologists Marija Gimbutas and Algirdas Julius Greimas emphasized that Žemyna was never a abstract construct, but a living sacred organism: the soil is her body, the stones her bones, the springs her blood, and the forests her hair.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

In the Baltic worldview, Žemyna holds a central position alongside the celestial deities Dievas and Perkūnas. As Mother Earth (*Žemė Motina*), her domain encompasses soil fertility, crop growth, cattle breeding, domestic prosperity, and the reception of the deceased back into her womb.

She is intimately linked to Perkūnas: in spring, Perkūnas's thunder and lightning "unlocks" and fertilizes the cold earth. Before the first spring thunder, Baltic custom strictly forbade disturbing the soil—digging, striking, or cutting the earth was considered a sacrilege against the resting mother.

## 3. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

Rituals dedicated to Žemyna were characterized by deep reverence and grounding:

- **The Grounding Rite**: Before sharpening the plow, farmers pressed both palms into the moist furrow until feeling the pulse of roots and earthworms, seeking permission to work the soil softly.
- **Libations and Offerings**: The first draught of ale from the horn and the first slice of freshly baked rye bread were poured and buried directly into the earth as offerings.
- **Rites of Birth and Passing**: Newborn infants were laid briefly upon the bare earth to secure Žemyna's blessing. At death, prayers were offered asking Žemyna to receive the body gently.

## 4. Folklore, Dainas, and Oral Tradition

In oral tradition, Žemyna is depicted as a generous yet exacting mother who remembers every crumb returned to her soil. Folktales recount that when crops failed, elders believed the soil had been starved of stories, gathering under the open sky to recite ancestral lineages and feed the land with words.

Folk songs (*Dainos*) celebrate her abundance:
> "Žemynėle, floral mother,  
> You bear blossoms and nurture grain.  
> Receive our humble offering,  
> Bless the labor of our hands."

## 5. Sacred Geography and Cultural Legacy

Žemyna's veneration left prominent marks across the Baltic landscape. Sacred hillforts such as Rambynas Hill, Kernavė, and Samogitian hill-sanctuaries served as meeting points of earth and sky cults. Contemporary ethnocultural communities continue the tradition of honoring the first furrow of spring and maintaining reverence for the soil.
