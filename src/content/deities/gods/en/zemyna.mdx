## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

The name Žemyna derives from Lithuanian *žemė* (“earth, soil”), from Proto-Balto-Slavic *źémē* and ultimately the Proto-Indo-European earth root *dʰéǵʰōm* (cf. Latin *humus*, Greek *chamaí*). She is the Lithuanian and Prussian earth goddess who personifies fertile soil as sacred mother, giver and nurturer of life. Name variants and diminutives include *Žemelė*, *Žemynėlė*, and related forms; the Latvian functional counterpart is *Zemes māte*.

Historical records place her among the best-documented Lithuanian deities of the early modern period:
- **Jan Łasicki (*De Diis Samagitarum*, late 16th c.; printed 1615)**: First written mention of Žemyna as earth goddess.
- **Matthäus Prätorius (*Deliciae Prussicae*, late 17th c.)**: Describes her as sister of Žemėpatis who makes the earth fertile; records the household ritual *žemyneliauti* (libation and duty paid first to the earth goddess).
- **Daniel Klein (17th c.)**: Compares her with Roman Ceres.
- Later ethnographic notes (e.g. Brodowski) and folklore continue her cult in agrarian and funeral practice.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

Žemyna occupies the chthonic–earth middle of Baltic cosmology beside the celestial powers Dievas and Perkūnas. As Mother Earth (*Žemė Motina*), her sphere is especially the cultivated and harvested earth: soil fertility, grain and fruit growth, livestock welfare, household prosperity, and care for the dead.

Spring thunder and rain from Perkūnas are understood to unlock and fertilize her; folk custom forbade plowing or sowing before the first spring thunder, when the earth would otherwise remain barren. She gives life generously yet punishes neglectful farming.

**Relations:** Žemėpatis (brother / homestead co-guardian in Prätorius); Žemininkas and related male earth guardians (distinct male counterparts); Perkūnas (spring thunder fertilizes the earth); Latvian *Zemes māte* (functional equivalent); compared with Roman Ceres.

## 3. Character and Visuals

Žemyna is imagined primarily as the living earth itself rather than as an anthropomorphic portrait deity. Cultivated soil is her active body and power; she is a generous yet exacting mother who nourishes crops, animals, and people and receives them again at death.

Sacred attributes associated with her cult include grain, beer made from grain, bread, and (in ethnographic reconstruction) flat granary stones treated as keepers of grain and cattle. Ritual address uses affectionate diminutives such as *Žemynėlė*.

## 4. Natural Habitat, Related Locations

Her cult is enacted in the homestead and in the fields rather than at named monumental temples. Offerings are poured or buried into the soil at the start and end of farm work, at birth, marriage, and burial. Flat granary stones and field or yard stones can function as points of contact with her power. Latvian parallel practice records milk and related offerings at fenced trees or stones for *Zemes māte*.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

- ***Žemyneliauti***: At feasts and major farm tasks, the head of household first pours drink onto the ground for Žemynėlė, then drinks; without her, nothing is begun.
- **Ale and bread**: Libation of ale (often from a *kaušelis*) and offering or burying of bread—including bread from the previous harvest before new sowing—are central agrarian rites.
- **Seasonal farm offerings**: Sacrifice and thanks at the spring opening and autumn close of agricultural work; harvest beer drunk in her honor.
- **Birth**: The newborn is briefly laid on Mother Earth so she may accept and embrace the child.
- **Death / wake**: Bread and meat pieces (sources note three bites) and ale are cast or poured to the earth, asking her to be gracious to the dead person’s soul.
- **Animals**: Pig or piglet offerings are linked with earth cultivation in early modern ethnographic notes; poultry may accompany *žemyneliauti* feasts.
- **Prayer formula** (Prätorius / later folk): *Žemynele, Žedkellei…* — asking her to bloom with rye and bless the household’s work.
