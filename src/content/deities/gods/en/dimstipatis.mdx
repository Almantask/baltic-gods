## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Dimstipatis is the Lithuanian lord of the homestead and household fire. His name is formed from *dimstis* (yard, farmstead, holding) and *pats* (lord, master).

Written attestation begins in the late sixteenth century:
- **1583 Jokūbas Lavinskis (Jakub Ławiński), Jesuit letter to Paul Campana**: First mention, as *Dimstipan*, among the Aukštaitians (including the Žemaitkiemis / Ukmergė district), with offerings reported.
- **1604–1605 Jesuit reports**: Describe Dimstipatis as a house god to whom seasonal sacrifices are made; some call him a fire god who keeps the dwelling from burning.
- **Eighteenth century Stanislovas Rostovskis**: Repeats the cult in *interpretatio romana* as a household *Lar*.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

Dimstipatis rules the domestic sphere: the homestead buildings, the hearth, property, and the family living under one roof. He is invoked as the power that guards the house from fire and secures the household’s goods and kin. An offering to him is understood to cover property, fire, hearth, and family together. Scholarship places him among the mid-level *patis* lords who govern a domain and its lesser spirits — the house counterpart to the field lord **Laukpatis**.

**Relations:**
- **Laukpatis** (LT): counterpart lord of the cultivated field; Dimstipatis holds the house as Laukpatis holds the field.
- **Pagirinis** (LT): separately named household being in the same Jesuit domestic cluster; Dimstipatis stands over the broader house-and-fire domain.
- **Lauksargis** (LT): field-sphere guardian under the Laukpatis complex, not a house theonym.
- **Mājas kungs / mājas gars** (LV): functional parallel — the Latvian house-lord and hearth guardian equated with Dimstipatis in comparative scholarship.

## 3. Character and Visuals

- **Cult focus**: The household hearth (*židinys*) is the centre of his rites; leftovers and wash-water from sacrificial meals are poured into the fire.
- **Latvian parallel forms (*Mājas kungs*)**: May appear as a man or woman in white clothes, or in animal shape as a toad, snake, or beetle; folk belief also seats him behind the stove, under the floor, in a yard-edge tree, or at a field stone.

## 4. Natural Habitat, Related Locations

His sanctuary is the inhabited farmstead and its hearth, not a remote grove cult.

- **Aukštaitija homestead hearth (Žemaitkiemis / Ukmergė district)**: Earliest named cult area in Lavinskis’s 1583 report.
- **Latvian *Mājas kungs* loci**: Old hearth ruins, the yard edge (*sētmalā*), a sacred tree, or a stone in the field where offerings are left.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

Jesuit and later reports describe several sacrificial patterns:

- **Annual house feast with black hens**: Once a year the household holds a feast and offers two black hens (Lavinskis notes small chicks); the meat is eaten, and bones, scraps, and the water used to wash the feast dishes are poured into the hearth and burned. Leftovers must not remain for the next day.
- **Rooster offering**: Another report names a cock sacrificed to the fire god; the meat is eaten and the bones thrown into the hearth.
- **Housewives’ piglet rite**: Some hold him to be the mistresses’ god; women stand in a circle holding a piglet, the eldest kills it, and they alone eat the meat — children are barred from the rite.
- **Latvian *Mājas kungs* calendar offerings**: Cock (sometimes buried), beer or spirits poured at the sacred spot, and food left at Jurģi and Miķeļi; the first morsel of a meal may be set aside for the house-lord, and the spirit may be invited when a new house is entered.
