## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

In the archaic polyphony of North-Eastern Lithuania, sacred refrains and ritual vocables formed the foundation of communal sound:
* **Ritual Refrains & Vocables**: The sacred syllables and recurrent refrains—*Lado, Laduola, Tatato, Čiūto, Totari, Dūno, Rūta*—represent archaic Baltic ritual vocables (*garsažodžiai*) preserved through generations of oral singing.
* **Chronicle & Folkloric Recording**: Early chronicles and later ethnographic compendiums documented these repetitive formulas; monumental field documentation compiled thousands of polyphonic songs across Aukštaitija.
* **Latvian Parallels**: Corresponds to traditional Latvian ritual cries and daina refrains, such as *Līgo!* and *Rūtē!* in midsummer Jāņi songs, *Rotā!* in spring calls, and *Totari!* or *Kaladū!* in winter masking songs.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, & Sacred Domain

In Baltic cosmology, collective song was not mere aesthetic entertainment, but an active ritual instrument for sustaining cosmic harmony (*darna*):
* **Opening Sacred Acoustic Space**: The intricate interplay of voices creates an acoustic threshold where human community aligns directly with the natural and celestial order.
* **The Concept of Darna**: The core ethical and cosmological concept of Baltic spirituality—*darna* (harmony, concord, mutual agreement)—is realized acoustically through synchronized polyphonic performance.
* **Seasonal & Agricultural Regulation**: Specific song forms accompanied critical stages of the agrarian calendar, including springtime field preparation, rye harvesting, flax retting, and wedding transitions.
* **Invocation of Sacred Flora**: Songs repeatedly weave incantatory references to venerated plants and trees, most notably the linden (*liepa*), rue (*rūta*), and flowering flax (*linelis*).

## 3. Character & Visuals

The sacred presence of the polyphonic tradition is embodied collectively within the circle of performers:
* **The Circle of Singers**: Performed exclusively in disciplined female ensembles where distinct musical roles are distributed among the lead singer (*rinkėja*), the partner answering (*panuokėja*), and the drone singer (*tūrintoja*).
* **Sonic Dissonance & Resonance**: Characterized by sharp, unresolved secondal intervals that generate shimmering acoustic beats and a meditative, resonant drone.
* **Ceremonial Dress & Instruments**: Accompanied visually by pristine white linen headscarves (*nuometai*), woven sashes, and archaic instrumental sets including panpipes (*skudučiai*), wooden trumpets (*daudytės*), and five-string zithers (*kanklės*).

## 4. Natural Habitat & Related Locations

The sacred heartland of Baltic polyphony is rooted in the landscape of North-Eastern Lithuania:
* **Aukštaitijos Heartland**: The historic singing districts of Biržai, Kupiškis, Pasvalys, and Rokiškis, where the oral lineage of sutartinės was meticulously maintained.
* **Village Rye Fields & Flax Ponds**: Ripening fields and water-retting ponds where women gathered during seasonal labour to interlock their voices across the open meadows.
* **Midsummer Hilltops**: Ritual singing mounds and sacred riverbanks where sunset songs greeted the celestial cycles during the solstice.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, & Cult Rites

Baltic communities maintained rigorous customary rules governing polyphonic song:
* **Gender & Ritual Exclusivity**: Vocal sutartinės were traditionally sung exclusively by women and maidens, maintaining sacred communal discipline and mutual acoustic attentiveness.
* **Seasonal Appropriateness**: Songs were restricted to their rightful calendar seasons; harvest songs were never performed in winter, nor wedding chants during Lent.
* **Unbroken Sonic Flow**: Performers maintained continuous, cyclical repetition without pause, creating an unbroken sonic ribbon that encircled and sanctified the gathered gathering.
