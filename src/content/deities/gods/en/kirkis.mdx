## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Kirkis (recorded in historical chronicles as *Chyurchas*, *Kircho*, and cognate with Old Prussian *Curche*) is the Baltic deity of cereal harvest, grain abundance, and the vegetal fertility of the soil.

Medieval chronicles and Baltic comparative research document this harvest deity:

* **Chronicle Attestations**: Documented in Jan Długosz's *Annales seu Cronicae incliti Regni Poloniae* (1455, Lib. X, BRMŠ Vol. 1, pp. 556, 563): *Chyurchas, quem Cererem deam frugum credebant, cui peractis messibus gratiarum actionis ergo pecora et iumenta mactabant* ("Chyurchas, whom they believed to be Ceres, goddess of the fruits of the earth, to whom upon completing the harvest they sacrificed cattle in thanksgiving"). Maciej Stryjkowski's *Kronika Polska...* (1582, BRMŠ Vol. 2, pp. 547, 560) records *Kircho / Kirkas* as the deity presiding over grain yields and autumn celebrations.
* **Old Prussian Precursor**: Recorded in the Treaty of Christburg (1249, BRMŠ Vol. 1, p. 235) as *Curche* (*Curcho*), an annual harvest idol fashioned from collected crops and venerated at harvest completion.
* **Baltic Linguistic Etymology**: Derived from Proto-Baltic *\*Kurk-as* / *\*Kurk-is* (with East Baltic palatalization *Kiurkis / Čiurkis* -> *Kirkis*), philologically analyzed by Rolandas Kregždys from Baltic *\*k̂er-* / *\*kerp-* ("to harvest, reap, cut") and Lithuanian *kurkti / kirkėti* ("to swell, ripen with fertile grain") and *kurklys* ("mole cricket", traditional indicator of fertile cropland).
* **Comparative Folklore Parallels**: Corresponds directly to the Latvian grain deity **Jumis** (LD 28523-0, LD 28527-0, LD 28537-0) and agricultural mother spirits (**Lauka māte**, **Druvu māte**).

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

In Baltic agricultural religion, Kirkis governs the final ripening of cereal crops and the preservation of food abundance:

### Guardian of Ripe Grain, Harvest Bounty, and Seed Life-Force

* **Protector of Cereal Crops**: Presides over rye, barley, wheat, and oats, ensuring the grain heads fill completely and resist seasonal rot.
* **Keeper of the Harvest Sheaf**: Embodies the concentrated vegetative life-force that retreats into the final unharvested sheaf of grain (*paskutinis pėdas* / *jumis*).
* **Granary and Winter Food Preserver**: Safeguards harvested grain stores from winter frost, dampness, and vermin throughout the cold season.

## 3. Character and Visuals

Baltic agrarian tradition conceives Kirkis through vegetal effigy and field symbols:

* **The Harvest Sheaf Idol (*Pėdo stabas*)**: Fashioned as an anthropomorphic harvest figure woven from unthreshed ears of ripe rye, adorned with autumn flowers and woven linen bands.
* **The Sickle and Double-Ear (*Dviguba vārpa*)**: Symbolized by the iron harvesting sickle (*pjautuvas*) and twin-grained cereal heads.
* **The Autumn Loaf and Beer Horn**: Represented by freshly baked whole-rye sourdough bread and foaming harvest beer.

## 4. Natural Habitat and Related Locations

The sacred terrain associated with Kirkis spans cultivated grainfields, threshing barns, and village feast grounds:

* **Cultivated Rye and Barley Fields**: The open arable croplands where the ceremonial first and last grain cuts were performed.
* **Threshing Barns (*Jaujos ir klojimai*)**: The log threshing floors where the harvest sheaf was dried, celebrated, and preserved.
* **Communal Harvest Feast Grounds**: Traditional farmstead yards where autumn thanksgiving banquets (*Sambariai*) took place.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Folk Practices

Ethnographic records and historical accounts document established harvest rituals:

* **The Cutting of the Final Sheaf**: Reapers left a patch of standing grain uncut, braided the stalks into a ceremonial sheaf (*Kirkiui / Jumiui*), and carried it home with harvest songs.
* **Autumn Thanksgiving Feasts (*Sambariai / Pabaigtuvės*)**: Pouring fresh beer libations onto the threshing floor and dedicating the first hot loaf of whole-grain bread in gratitude for the harvest.
* **Spring Sowing Reincorporation**: The grain from the harvest effigy was carefully threshed in spring and mixed into the sowing seed to transfer fertility into the new crop.
