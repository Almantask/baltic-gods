## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Sutvaras (historically recorded as *Sotwaros* and *Sutvaris*) is the Baltic deity of livestock herds, animal fecundity, and cosmogonic form-shaping.

Sixteenth-century historical chronicles and Baltic linguistic research document this tradition:

* **Sixteenth-Century Chronicle Attestations**: Documented in Maciej Stryjkowski's *Kronika Polska, Litewska, Żmódzka i wszystkiej Rusi* (1582, reprinted in BRMŠ Vol. 2) with the explicit record: *Sotwaros, wszytkiego bydła bóg, któremu pstre kapłony ofiarowali, aby bydło różnej maści i zdrowe chował* ("Sotwaros, deity of all cattle, to whom mottled capons were sacrificed so that he would keep the cattle healthy and of varied color"). Jan Łasicki's treatise *De diis Samagitarum caeterorumque Sarmatarum et falsorum Christianorum* (composed c. 1582, published Basel 1615) likewise includes *Sotwaros* within the Samogitian pantheon catalogue.
* **Baltic Linguistic Etymology**: Rooted in the Lithuanian verb *sutverti* ("to create, fashion, enclose, give form") and the related nominal form *tvaras* / *aptvaras* ("livestock pen, fold, enclosure"), derived from Proto-Baltic *\*twer-* and Proto-Indo-European *\*twer-* / *\*tur-* ("to enclose, shape, compress"). In archaic Lithuanian, the noun *sutvėrimas* preserves a dual meaning denoting both "creation / structure" and "living creature / cattle".
* **Cosmogonic Parallels**: Corresponds conceptually in comparative Baltic mythography to the demiurgic capacity of the supreme deity (*Dievas* / *Radītājs*), who encloses ordered cultural space (*Dieva sētiņa*) out of primeval chaos.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

In Baltic agrarian economy and archaic cosmology, Sutvaras operates across two interrelated spheres:

### Patron of Livestock Herds and Shaper of Physical Forms

* **Guardianship of Cattle Herds**: Preserves the health, vital strength, reproduction, and coat coloration of domestic cattle, sheep, and horses.
* **Master of the Enclosure (*Tvaras*)**: Protects the livestock pen and fold against nocturnal predators, diseases, and harsh weather.
* **The Demiurgic Shaper**: Functions on the cosmogonic plane as the divine architect who gives distinct form, structural limits, and functional beauty to raw physical substance.

## 3. Character and Visuals

Baltic folk tradition embodies Sutvaras through pastoral and structural symbols:

* **Livestock Horns and Pastoral Folds**: Represented by polished cattle horns, timber pen fences (*tvarai*), and woven wicker enclosures.
* **Mottled Sacrificial Feathers**: Symbolized by the variegated, piebald plumage of mottled roosters dedicated during pastoral rites.
* **The Shaper's Tools**: Evoked through the potter's shaping hand, measuring cords, and boundary posts that establish spatial order.

## 4. Natural Habitat and Related Locations

The sacred terrain of Sutvaras encompasses pastoral pastures, homestead enclosures, and ritual mounds:

* **Pasture Enclosures and Byres (*Tvarai ir tvartai*)**: The domestic cattle folds and village pastures across Samogitia and Aukštaitija.
* **Sacred Creation Mounds**: Earthen hilltop ridges in Samogitia traditionally dedicated to communal beginnings and pastoral blessings.
* **Baltic Meadow Pastures**: Expansive river valley grazing meadows where livestock herds gathered under divine protection.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Folk Practices

Historical chronicles describe precise pastoral sacrifices dedicated to Sutvaras:

* **Sacrifice of Mottled Capons**: Maciej Stryjkowski (1582) records that cattle breeders sacrificed piebald/mottled capons (*pstre kapłony*) burned before the hearth fire (*przed ogniem*) to secure healthy, multicolored herds.
* **Enclosure Blessings (*Tvaro šventinimas*)**: Uttering protective invocations and circling newly constructed livestock folds with burning embers before driving herds inside.
* **Pasture Invocations at Spring Turnout (*Jurginės*)**: Dedicating the first milk and bread crusts to the earth when cattle were turned out to open pastures for the first time in spring.
