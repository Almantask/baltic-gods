## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Pjūties Dievas (also *Pjovėjas*; Latvian: *Pļaujas dievs*, *Pļāvējs*) is the traditional Baltic personification of the grain harvest (*pjūtis*, *rugiapjūtė*) and agricultural completion. The name derives from the Proto-Baltic verbal root *\*pliau-* / *\*pjau-* ("to cut, mow, reap"), cognate with Lithuanian *pjauti*, *pjūtis*, *pjovėjas*, and Latvian *pļaut*, *pļauja*, *pļāvējs*.

In historical agrarian tradition, the harvest was understood as a sacred threshold in the farming calendar:
- **Agrarian Customs & Folklore**: Recorded in Lithuanian and Latvian harvest songs (*rugiapjūtės dainos*, *pļaujas dainas*) and ethnographic descriptions of first reaping (*prapjovos*) and harvest-end feasts (*pabaigtuvės*, *apjumības*).
- **Spiritual Custodians of the Field**: While the physical labor of mowing was conducted by communal reapers (*talka*, *pjovėjai*), the sacred life of the harvested grain and offerings centered upon **Žemyna** (the Earth Mother) and **Jievaras** (the spirit of the field and grain, Latvian *Jumis*).

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

The harvest tradition marks the crucial turning point from summer vegetation to winter sustenance in Baltic cosmology:
- **Agrarian Cycle Closure**: Presides over the culmination of the agricultural year, when living crops are transformed into bread and stored grain.
- **Ritual Complementarity**: Operates alongside the spring emergence of Pergrubrijus; while spring ceremonies welcome the green sprouting of the earth, harvest rites express communal thanksgiving for completed growth.
- **Pantheon & Spirit Relations**: Closely connected to Žemyna, who receives the final sheaf offerings, and to the indwelling grain spirit Jievaras (or Jumis), who preserves vegetative fertility for the next planting.

## 3. Character and Visuals

Folklore and traditional agricultural life depict the reaper archetype through distinct customary symbols:
- **Iconographic Presence**: Envisioned as a diligent, weathered mower (*pjovėjas*) working in rhythm with the sun from dawn to dusk.
- **Wearables**: Associated with traditional white linen harvesting garb (*balti marškiniai*, *margos juostos*), historically worn by peasants on the ceremonial first and last days of reaping.
- **Tools & Attributes**: Wields the curved harvest sickle (*pjautuvas*) and scythe (*dalgis*), carrying the first sheaf (*diedas*) and the final ceremonial sheaf.
- **Sacred Symbols**: The braided clump of standing rye stalks (*Jievaro barzda*), the harvest wreath (*pabaigtuvių vainikas*), and wagons carrying the golden harvest to granaries.

## 4. Natural Habitat, Related Locations

The realm of the harvest spirit spans the fertile agricultural landscapes of the Baltic:
- **Cultivated Fields**: Open rye and barley fields (*rugių laukai*), boundary furrows, and communal threshing barns (*klojimai*, *rijas*).
- **Aukštaitija Rye Fields**: Historic cereal-growing lands of eastern and central Lithuania where elaborate *Jievaro barzda* ceremonies were preserved into the 20th century.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

Baltic harvest rituals combined communal labor, feasting, and sacred offerings:
- **Prapjovos (First Reaping)**: The ceremonial commencement of harvest, where the first cut sheaf (*diedas*, *svečias*) was brought home and placed in the corner of honor.
- **Jievaro Barzda (Braiding the Last Stalks)**: At the conclusion of reaping, harvesters left a patch of uncut rye standing in the field, weeded its base, braided the ears into a decorative knot, and placed bread and salt underneath with the formula: *"You gave to me, Earth, and I give back to you."*
- **Pabaigtuvės & Harvest Wreath**: Weaving the last ears into a harvest wreath (*pabaigtuvių vainikas*), carried by reapers with songs (*sambaris*) to the master of the farmstead in exchange for hospitality, cheese, and brewed beer.
