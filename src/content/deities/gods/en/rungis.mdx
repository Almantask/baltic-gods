## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Rungis (reflected in Baltic agrarian folklore and 19th-century historiography, functioning alongside the authentically attested Latvian *Mēslu māte*) represents the Baltic agrarian spirit presiding over manure composting, soil fertilization, and organic nutrient replenishment.

Historical sources and comparative ethnographic studies document the tradition:

* **Latvian Daina Tradition (*Latvju dainas*, ed. Krišjānis Barons)**: Authentically records *Mēslu māte* (the Mother of the Manure Heap) in ritual sheep-shearing songs where the first tuft of wool was offered into the compost heap (*Še, mēslu māte, tava daļa, neņem pate savu roku*, LD 29089-0).
* **19th-Century Historiography (Teodor Narbutt, 1835)**: Recorded *Rungis* in his pantheon survey, associating the figure with the fertility of agricultural farmsteads, grain processing, and agrarian labor.
* **Etymological Analysis**: The Lithuanian noun *rungas* designates a sturdy crossbar or stake of a wagon side frame, ladder, or dung sledge (*vežimo ar rogių gardos skersinis*), historically essential for hauling heavy loads of compost to fields during the spring manure-hauling bee (*mėšlavežis*).

The comparative role of Baltic manure and soil fertility deities is analysed by Norbertas Vėlius (*Baltų religijos ir mitologijos šaltiniai*, Vol. 2) and Pēteris Šmits (*Latviešu tautas ticējumi*).

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

In Baltic agricultural cosmology, soil exhaustion was a constant threat, making the recycling of livestock manure the vital turning point of the annual fertility cycle.

### Sovereign of Soil Enrichment and the Spring Manure-Hauling Bee

* **Guardian of Composting Fermentation**: Oversees the decomposition of winter barn bedding, ensuring that compost generates fertilizing warmth without burning seeds.
* **Presider over the Manure-Hauling Bee (*Mėšlavežio talka*)**: Blesses the communal spring labor when village farmsteads united to haul compost onto ploughed fallow fields.
* **Ally of Earth and Field Deities**: Works alongside Žemėpatis (earth master), Lauksargis (field guardian), and the Latvian Jumis to guarantee a bounteous autumn harvest.

## 3. Character and Visuals

Baltic agrarian folklore portrays the fertilizer spirit through earthy, hardworking iconography:

* **Sturdy Agrarian Elder**: Conceived as a muscular, earth-stained elder dressed in durable work clothes, standing amidst the farmstead compost grounds.
* **Carved Ash Manure Fork**: Carries a three-tined carved wooden manure fork (*mėšlašakės*) polished smooth from decades of agricultural use.
* **Leather Work Smock**: Clothed in a heavy untanned leather apron over rough homespun woollen trousers stained with rich black soil.
* **Soil-Brown Gaze and Mossy Beard**: Features deep earthen-brown eyes and a bristly, straw-flecked beard reflecting the vitality of composted soil.

## 4. Natural Habitat and Related Locations

The sacred space of Rungis encompasses the farmstead midden and newly enriched agricultural soils:

* **Farmstead Compost Middens (*Mėšlidės*)**: The designated compost yard behind traditional Baltic barns where organic fertilizer was accumulated over winter.
* **Ploughed Fallow Lands (*Pūdymai*)**: The open spring fields waiting for manure spreading and harrowing before rye planting.
* **Stable Thresholds and Barnyards**: The cattle pens and sheepfolds where livestock bedding was gathered.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Folk Practices

Baltic farming communities observed dedicated spring fertilization and livestock rituals:

* **First Shearing Manure Offering**: Tossing the first lock of freshly shorn sheep's wool into the dung heap with spoken prayers to ensure flock health and rich compost.
* **Manure-Hauling Feast (*Mėšlavežio pabaigtuvės*)**: Celebrating the end of manure hauling with communal beer libations and shared bread at the edge of the enriched field.
* **Ploughshare Consecration**: Sprinkling a handful of fertile compost over the iron ploughshare before breaking new soil in spring.
