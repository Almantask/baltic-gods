## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Maro Deivė (plural: *Maro deivės*; Latvian: *Mēra māte*) is the Baltic personification of pestilence, epidemic disease, and collective mortality. The name derives from Proto-Indo-European *\*mer-* / Proto-Baltic *\*māras* ("plague", "pestilence", "death"), cognate with Lithuanian *maras*, Latvian *mēris*, and Sanskrit *māra-*.

Historical records and folkloric collections document the plague deity across the Baltic region:
- **1571/1576 Prussian Administrative Records**: Documents from the Labguva (Labiau) region recorded in the report of amtmon to Georg Christoph von Kreytzen preserve the propitiatory cult of *Diedewaythe* (*Didė Dievaitė*, "Great Goddess"), where Curonian Lagoon fishermen conducted communal sacrificial rites to halt deadly epidemics.
- **19th–20th Century Folklore Collections**: Documented in extensive Lithuanian mythological legends (*sakmės*) collected by Jonas Basanavičius and indexed by Jonas Balys, describing the visits of the plague maiden (*Maro merga*, *Maro panelė*) traveling along roads from foreign lands.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

Maro Deivė occupies the chthonic and liminal realms of Baltic cosmology, governing collective epidemics and regional mortality:
- **Collective vs. Individual Mortality**: Distinct in function from Giltinė, who takes individuals at their appointed hour, Maro Deivė strikes entire villages and settlements simultaneously.
- **Atmospheric Travel**: Travels along roadways, boundaries, and rivers, entering settlements through open windows, keyholes, and boundary fences.
- **Pantheon & Creature Relations**: Operates in the chthonic sphere alongside Giltinė; arrives riding upon a white horse (*baltas žirgas*) or in a horse-drawn carriage (*karieta*).

## 3. Character and Visuals

Folklore traditions describe Maro Deivė with distinct spectral iconography:
- **Appearance**: Appears as a tall, slender woman or young maiden (*balta pana*); possesses a pale complexion (*blyški kaip mirtis*) or a darkened visage.
- **Hair**: Depicted with loose, flowing, or unbraided hair (*paleistos kasos*).
- **Wearables**: Clad in white linen garments and flowing robes (*balti rūbai*, *baltos drobės*), evoking burial shrouds.
- **Accessories**: Wields and waves a red cloth or scarf (*raudona skara*, *skepeta*), spreading infectious contagion wherever the fabric flutters.
- **Mount**: Rides a pale or white horse across fields and village outskirts.

## 4. Natural Habitat, Related Locations

Maro Deivė is linked with liminal transit zones and historic epidemic boundaries:
- **Liminal Geography**: Crossroads (*kryžkelės*), village gates, settlement borders, bridges, and desolate roads.
- **Samogitian Plague Barriers & Marked Mounds**: Historical crossroads and plague burial grounds (*markapiai*) across Samogitia where quarantine boundaries and wayside crosses were erected to halt contagion.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

Baltic communities practiced specific communal defense rites and evasion strategies during outbreaks:
- **Boundary Smoke Rites**: Communities lit perimeter bonfires burning pungent herbs, tar, and protective materials at village borders to turn away the plague.
- **Protective Textiles**: Weaving white "one-night cloth" (*vienadienė drobė*) between sunset and dawn by village maidens, stretched across entrance roads or hung on roadside crosses.
- **Evasion Customs**: Sealing chimney flues, observing silence at night when calls were heard, and establishing quarantine burials outside consecrated churchyards.
