## 1. Etymology & Historical Chronicles

Auschauts is the Old Prussian deity of health, physical wholeness, and recovery from sickness. Linguistically, Baltic philology derives the theonym from the prefix *au-* ("away, off") and the verbal root *šauti* ("to shoot, drive away"), designating him as the one who dispels or drives away illness from the human body. He is also connected to the Old Prussian verb *aushauditwei* ("to trust, rely upon"), reflecting communal reliance upon divine benevolence for physical well-being.

The deity is recorded in sixteenth-century historical chronicles documenting the religious traditions of the Old Prussians and Sudovians. In the 1530 *Constitutiones Synodales*, promulgated for the dioceses of Sambia and Pomesania, he is recorded as *Auschlavis*, the god of the sick and the healthy (*der krancken und gesunden Gott*). The mid-sixteenth-century *Sudauer Büchlein* (*Sudovian Book*) records *Auschauts* as the divine guardian who watches over the sick and the sound (*bewarhet die krancken und gesunden*). Renaissance chroniclers, beginning with Jan Malecki in his 1551 *Libellus de sacrificiis et idolatria veterum Borussorum*, equated Auschauts with the Greco-Roman god of medicine, Aesculapius.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, & Sacred Domain

In Old Prussian religion, Auschauts presides over bodily integrity, convalescence, and the preservation of life against physical affliction. He embodies the principle of restorative vitality, invoked when individuals suffer from illness, fevers, or the threat of epidemic disease.

Within the divine hierarchy recorded in the *Sudovian Book* and Renaissance chroniclers' catalogues, Auschauts occupies a prominent third position directly following the supreme celestial ruler *Occopirmus* and the god of light *Swayxtix*, preceding the aquatic and terrestrial deities *Autrimpus*, *Potrimpus*, *Perkūnas*, and *Peckols*. Functionally, Auschauts represents the Old Prussian manifestation of broader Baltic healing traditions (*tautas dziedniecība* / *liaudies medicina*), sharing sacred space with folk incantations, herbal knowledge, and curative spring waters.

## 3. Character & Visuals

Auschauts manifests as the benevolent guardian of human vitality, physical strength, and convalescence. Historical chronicles record him as an attentive patron to whom communities directed supplications for health and recovery during times of pestilence and bodily frailty.

## 4. Natural Habitat & Related Locations

The veneration of Auschauts was distributed across historical Old Prussian territories, particularly the Sambian peninsula (Samland), Natangia, Sudovia, and Warmia. His cultic environment centered on the natural sanctuaries of the Prussian landscape:

* **Sacred Groves (*Alkai*)**: Enclosed woodland sanctuaries where Prussian priests and communities gathered for seasonal prayers and restorative health supplications.
* **Curative Springs (*Šaltiniai*)**: Freshwater and mineral springs flowing near sanctuary groves, where the sick washed afflicted limbs and eyes to seek physical recovery.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, & Cult Practices

Prussian religious life incorporated communal and household supplications dedicated to preserving health and overcoming bodily afflictions:

* **Healing Invocations**: Sick individuals, families, and ritual officiants (*Wurschayti*, *Waidelotten*, *Tulissones*) offered formal prayers to Auschauts during communal gatherings to preserve the physical health of the community and dispel disease.
* **Votive Offerings**: Baltic healing customs at sacred groves and healing springs included offerings of clean freshwater, white linen cloths, beeswax, bread, grain, honey, and coins deposited at sanctuary sites to obtain recovery.
