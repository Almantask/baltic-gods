## 1. Etymology & Historical Chronicles

Curcho (Old Prussian: *Curcho / Curche / Kurko*; Lithuanian: *Kurkė / Kurkas*; Latvian: *Kurķis*) is the Old Prussian harvest deity and the personification of grain vitality, first-fruits, and food abundance. The theonym is linguistically connected to the Baltic root *\*kurk-* (attested in Lithuanian *kurkti* "to croak, swell, call forth", *kurti* "to create, ignite, establish", and Latvian *kurkt*), signifying swelling vegetative vitality and the concentrated reproductive force of the crop.

As one of the earliest documented Baltic deities in written history, Curcho is recorded in medieval treaties and early modern chronicles:

* **Treaty of Christburg (*Christburger Vertrag*, 1249)**: Oldest historical attestation, in which the Teutonic Order required newly converted Old Prussians to renounce the annual manufacture and worship of the harvest idol *Curche* (*"Idolum Curche, quod annuatim collectis frugibus consueverant confingere et pro deo colere"*).
* **Sudovian Book (*Sudauer Büchlein*, c. 1520–1530)**: Lists *Curche* as the deity presiding over food and drink (*der Speise und des Trankes Gott*), invoked to ensure full grain bins and family sustenance.
* **Simon Grunau (*Preussische Chronik*, c. 1526)**: Notes the autumn harvest feast dedicated to Curcho, where newly brewed beer and fresh bread were offered before communal consumption.
* **Christoph Hartknoch (*Alt- und neues Preussen*, 1684)**: Analyzes the custom of fashioning the last harvested sheaf into a human-shaped effigy, carried through the fields to preserve grain fertility for the following season.
* **Comparative Baltic Context**: Parallels the Lithuanian harvest personification **Rugių Boba** / **Jumis** and the Latvian **Jumis**, embodying the life-bearing kernel that bridges one harvest to the next.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, & Sacred Domain

In Old Prussian religion, Curcho presides over the culmination of the agricultural cycle, the preservation of seed vitality, and the food security of the clan.

### Sovereign of Grain Vitality & First-Fruits

* **Protector of the Sown Harvest**: Governs the reproductive energy within wheat, rye, and barley, ensuring that seed planted in the soil yields bountiful ears.
* **Master of the Food & Drink Economy**: Regulates the staple reserves of the household—flour chests, bread dough, malt, and fermented ale.
* **Theology of First-Fruits Repayment**: Operates on strict ritual reciprocity; the harvest was understood as a divine loan, and no human could taste the new grain until the first portion was returned to Curcho.
* **Guardian of the Granary Threshold**: Resides symbolically within the last harvested sheaf brought into the granary, keeping vermin, rot, and blight away from winter grain stores.

## 3. Character & Visuals

Historical chronicles and folkloric survivals describe Curcho through symbolic effigy and agricultural forms:

* **Harvest Effigy Form**: Manifested annually as a tall, human-shaped idol fashioned from golden rye straw and woven grain ears, dressed in rustic linen garments.
* **Facial Characteristics**: Rendered in straw-sculpted features with crown-like woven barley awns, bright amber-beaded eyes, and braided wheat tresses.
* **Emblematic Regalia**: Adorned with double-headed wheat ears (*Jumis* analog), a harvest sickle, a wooden threshing flail, and a birchbark beer flagon.
* **Processional Presence**: Carried aloft on decorated wooden poles through the harvested fields, surrounded by singing reapers and musicians playing pipes and horns.

## 4. Natural Habitat & Related Locations

The sacred domain of Curcho centers upon the ancient agricultural plains of Old Prussia:

* **Pomesanian & Pogesanian Harvest Plains**: Historic arable fields around Christburg (Dzierzgoń), Elbing (Elbląg), and Malbork where the 1249 chronicle prohibitions were recorded.
* **Threshing Floors & Granaries (*Jauja / Kluonas*)**: The timber drying barns where the straw idol was permanently installed on the main crossbeam to watch over the grain bins.
* **Sambia & Natangia Field Altars**: Boundary stones where the first sheaves of autumn rye were laid and anointed with fresh beer.
* **Communal Feast Grounds**: Village greens where the entire settlement gathered after the final field was cut for the harvest thanksgiving banquet.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, & Folk Practices

Historical sources record detailed harvest ceremonies dedicated to Curcho:

* **Creation of the Harvest Idol (*Idolum Curche*)**: Reapers left the final stand of grain standing until the end of harvest, ceremonially cutting it to weave the straw figure containing the grain's living soul (*nuba*).
* **First-Fruits Dedications (*Primiciae*)**: The first loaf baked from the new flour and the first pitcher poured from the new brew were brought to the granary altar and offered to Curcho before the harvest feast began.
* **Animal Sacrifices**: Chroniclers record the offering of a black cock, ram, or goat at the threshing floor to sanctify the grain bins against spoilage.
* **Preservation of the Seed Sheaf**: The idol was kept safely in the granary throughout winter; in spring, its grains were mixed into the seed bag to convey vitality to the new sowing.
