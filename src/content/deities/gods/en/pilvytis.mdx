## 1. Etymology & Historical Chronicles

Pilvytis (historically attested in Old Prussian sources as *Pilnitis*, *Pilwitus*, and *Pilvittus*) is the ancient Baltic and Old Prussian god of wealth, harvest surplus, and overflowing granaries (*deus divitiarum*). Linguistically, comparative Baltic scholarship establishes that the authentic primary theonym is *Pilnitis*, derived from the Proto-Baltic root *\*piln-* (Old Prussian *pilnan*, Lithuanian *pilnas*, Latvian *pilns* — "full, abundant, overflowing") with the Baltic suffix *-īt-*, translating literally as "The Fulfiller / God of Abundance." The historical variant *Pilvytis* developed through 16th-century scribal phonetic adaptations and secondary folk association with *pilvas* ("belly"), which in archaic Baltic agricultural culture was an idiom for physical satiety, abundance, and bodily satisfaction.

The deity is extensively documented across sixteenth-century Prussian and Baltic chronicles:

* **Sudovian Book (*Sudauer Büchlein*, c. 1520–1530)**: Lists *Pilwitus* / *Pilnitis* as the deity who enriches the farmer and fills storage barns (*"Pilwitus machet reich und vberfluß"*).
* **Constitutiones Synodales (1530)**: Recorded by the Synods of Pomesania and Sambia as *Pilnitis* (*"Pilnitis der vberfluß machet"* — "Pilnitis who brings surplus").
* **Jan Malecki (*Libellus de sacrificiis*, 1551)**: Mentions *Piluitus* as the Baltic deity of riches, comparing him to the classical god of wealth, *Pluto*.
* **Alessandro Guagnini (1578)**: Documents in *Sarmatiae Europeae Descriptio* that beer libations were ritually offered to *Pilvytis* (*Pilwitowi*) during agricultural seasonal festivals to ensure wealth and prosperity.
* **Jonas Bretkūnas (1578–1580)**: Records in *Chronicon Borussiae* that farmers invoked the god of abundance (*gausumo dievas*) before spring sowing, praying for heavy grain ears and filled threshing barns (*klojimai*).
* **Jan Łasicki (*De diis Samagitarum*, 1582)**: Summarizes the deity's role as *"Pilvittum qui opulentos divitesque reddit"* (Pilvittus who makes people wealthy and prosperous).

In modern academic literature, the deity has been analyzed in *Baltų religijos ir mitologijos šaltiniai* (Vol. 2, ed. N. Vėlius), *Visuotinė lietuvių enciklopedija*, Rolandas Kregždys (*Baltų mitologemų etimologijos žodynas*, 2012; Vol. II *Sūduvių knygelė*, 2020), Vytautas Mažiulis (*Prūsų kalbos etimologijos žodynas*, Vol. 3), and Vladimir Toporov.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, & Sacred Domain

In the Baltic theological framework, Pilvytis embodies the vital economic threshold of harvest accumulation, ensuring that agricultural labor successfully translates into sustained household survival and material security through the winter.

### Multiplication of Stores & Protection from Scarcity

* **Granary Fullness**: Unlike celestial deities who governed atmospheric phenomena, Pilvytis was invoked directly within the domestic farmstead sphere to multiply threshed grain, flour, and seed corn.
* **Food Security**: His blessing protected granary bins (*aruodai*) from premature depletion, dampness, and rodent pests, ensuring that food reserves endured until the next harvest cycle.

### Divine Relations & Baltic Parallels

* **Prussian Terrestrial Triad**: Operates within the agrarian-fertility cycle alongside *Pergrubrius* (the bringer of spring greenery), *Patrimpas* (god of flowing waters, spring fertility, and grain crops), and *Kurke* (the grain spirit gathered in the final harvest sheaf).
* **Lithuanian Parallels**: Corresponds functionally to Lithuanian *Žemėpatis* (lord of the farmstead and cultivated earth) and *Dimstipatis* (master of the estate buildings and stores).
* **Latvian Parallel**: Corresponds functionally to the Latvian fertility and harvest spirit **Jumis**, who brings overflowing granaries (*pilnas klētis*).

## 3. Character & Visuals

In Baltic religious perception, Pilvytis was revered as a benevolent divine provider and the numinous presence of fullness:

* **Numinous Presence of Satiety**: Conceived not as an anthropomorphic statue, but as the invisible divine fulfiller residing within packed granaries and well-managed farmsteads.
* **Cultic Attributes**: Symbolically associated with overflowing grain bins (*aruodai*), heavy ears of ripe rye, wooden grain measures (*saikai / pūrai*), granary keys, and ceremonial beer ladles.

## 4. Natural Habitat & Related Locations

Pilvytis's veneration was centered across the historic agricultural heartlands of Western and Eastern Baltic lands:

* **Regional Geography**: Sambia (*Semba*), Natangia, Pomesania (*Pamedė*), and the Sudovian agricultural heartland (*Sūduva*).
* **Sacred Farmstead Loci**: Traditional timber granaries (*klėtys / svirnai*), grain bins (*aruodai*), and threshing barn floors (*klojimai*).

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, & Folk Practices

Historical chronicles record specific liturgical practices and household offerings dedicated to Pilvytis:

* **Spring Sowing Supplications & Beer Libations**: During the spring agricultural festival (*Pergrubrinių šventė*) and immediately prior to sowing, the housefather offered prayers to Pilvytis and poured beer libations (*atlieja alų*) onto the ground to ensure large ears of grain and abundant yields.
* **Granary First-Fruits Consecration**: When freshly threshed grain was poured into granary bins in late autumn, the first measure was consecrated to Pilvytis/Pilnitis at the granary threshold (*klėties slenkstis*) to ensure the stores would not empty before spring.
* **Votive Offerings**: Offerings of grain, newly ground flour, fresh harvest bread, and beer were placed in granaries and threshing barns.
