## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

In Latvian folk tradition, Mārtiņš represents an ancient agrarian and equine deity presiding over the onset of winter:
* **Historical Syncretism**: Named in connection with the feast of St. Martin of Tours (November 10, *Mārtiņi*), the figure preserves an archaic pre-Christian mythological layer governing harvest completion, stable protection, and seasonal threshold transition.
* **Folklore Documentation**: Chronicled extensively in Krišjānis Barons' canonical folk song corpus *Latvju dainas* (such as dainas LD 30215 and LD 30216), where the figure is directly addressed as a generous, horse-loving supernatural visitor.
* **Comparative Pantheon Position**: Analyzed by folklorist Pēteris Šmits and contemporary mythologists as the autumn counterpart to spring horse guardian *Ūsiņš*, marking the closing of the light half of the year.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, & Sacred Domain

Mārtiņš presides over the critical agricultural and pastoral turning point between late autumn and deep winter:
* **Guardian of Horses**: Serves as the supernatural patron and protector of domestic horses (*zirgu aizgādnis*) during the vulnerable indoor stabling season, shielding them from disease, nocturnal distress, and winter weakness.
* **Closing the Agricultural Year**: Signals the absolute cessation of outdoor plowing and field labor, the finish of grain threshing (*kulšana*) in barns, and the freezing of pasture turf.
* **Settlement of Accounts**: Historically established the legal and customary term for farmhands and laborers to conclude service contracts, settle annual debts, and receive winter provisions.

## 3. Character & Visuals

In traditional dainas and folk narratives, Mārtiņš is vividly personified with aristocratic and winter pastoral iconography:
* **The Noble Rider**: Portrayed as a wealthy rider (*bagāts vīrs*) arriving upon a sturdy white, dapple-grey, or bay stallion (*bērs kumeliņš*) whose mane sparkles with rime frost.
* **Winter Garments**: Cloaked in heavy, insulated northern traveling dress—a thick sheepskin coat (*kažoks*), warm woolen gloves, and a fur cap suited to driving sleet and frozen winds.
* **Sacred Herald**: Associated with the domestic rooster (*gailis*), often depicted in poetic texts as having vibrant red plumage, nine distinct combs, or bright yellow spurs.

## 4. Natural Habitat & Related Locations

The veneration of Mārtiņš focused upon key structural sanctuaries of the traditional Baltic homestead:
* **Horse Stables (*Zirgu stallis*)**: The timber stabling enclosures where work horses were sheltered and fed through the frozen months (`kurzeme-martins-festival-site`).
* **Threshing Barns (*Rija*)**: The heavy timber kiln-barns where the season's dried rye and barley were processed and winnowed before winter storage.
* **Thresholds and Gateways**: Sills, gates, and barn doors where protective marks and offerings secured the domestic perimeter against demonic marauders (*lietuvēns*).

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, & Cult Rites

The festival of *Mārtiņi* was observed with solemn sacrifices and celebratory communal practices:
* **Stable Rooster Sacrifice (*Gaiļa ziedošana*)**: Sacrificing a dedicated black or red rooster directly in the horse stalls on the eve of the festival, smearing blood on manger beams, cribs, and door frames to imbue horses with vitality and ward off hostile spirits.
* **Mumming Processions (*Mārtiņbērni*)**: Bands of masked revelers (*budēļi*, *ķekatas*) dressed as bears, cranes, goats, and wolves traveling from farm to farm, singing ritual blessings, testing household diligence, and demanding festive gifts.
* **Festive Meal**: Communal sharing of roasted rooster or goose, fresh barley beer (*kvass* or *alus*), warm loaves of sourdough rye, and rich blood puddings to celebrate harvest prosperity.
