## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Giltinė is the Lithuanian personification of death. Her name is derived from *gelti* (“to sting / pierce”) and related forms *geluonis* / *geltonis*, matching the folk belief that she kills by stinging with a poisonous tongue.

Written attestation begins in the late seventeenth century:
- **Matthäus Praetorius (*Deliciae Prussicae*, finished about 1690)**: First clear mention; he lists her among the household gods (*Namiszki Diewai*) beside Pykullis (Pikulas) and Magyla as figures of anger and misfortune.
- **Later Prussian–Lithuanian dictionaries (J. Brodowski, P. Ruigys)**: Call her a goddess of death and plague; G. Ostermeyer used *Giltinė* as a synonym for death, while K. G. Milkuss preferred to treat her as a plague goddess.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

Giltinė embodies the end of a human life. Her chief work is to watch when a person’s time has come and then to take that life — by stinging with her long tongue, by strangling, or, in later Christian-coloured imagery, by the scythe. She slips around houses where the sick lie; the dying may see her approach from the direction of the graveyard.

**Relations:**
- **Laima**: sister and counterpart; Laima opens life, Giltinė closes it. Folklore seats the sisters riding the same horse; Laima guards from the right, Giltinė harms from the left, and the two contend over human fortune.
- **Magyla**: named as her servant in the early scholarly lists that pair her with Praetorius’s anger-gods.
- **Maro deivės (plague goddesses)**: closely related figures; Lietuvininkai folklore often links Giltinė herself with plague and epidemic death, while other traditions keep a separate plague-maiden.
- **Owl (*pelėda*)**: night hoot as a death omen announcing her approach.
- **Dog**: barking when nobody is about as another herald of her coming.

Latvian tradition does not use the name Giltinė as a native cult figure; comparative death mothers there are *Veļu māte* / *Kapu māte* and the personified *Nāve*.

## 3. Character and Visuals

Folk tradition shows two layered images — an older Baltic death-woman and a later European “Grim Reaper” overlay:

- **Looks**: An elderly, ugly woman; very thin / withered (*liesa*, *sudžiūvusi*); long blue nose and blue-tinged face; long (sometimes forked) tongue filled with poison; long nails.
- **Origin transformation**: Once young, sociable, and as beautiful as Laima; after being deceitfully shut in a coffin for seven years she emerged withered and hideous.
- **Clothing**: White garments or a white shroud/cloak; later images add a black hooded cloak.
- **Accessories / weapons**: The venomous tongue as her primary “sting”; later Christian iconography adds a scythe and skeletal form (as in danse macabre and church sculpture).

## 4. Natural Habitat, Related Locations

Giltinė’s lore belongs to the domestic threshold of dying rather than to a single named sanctuary:
- **Sickbed and farmhouse**: She prowls houses with the ill and stands at the bedside when the hour arrives.
- **Graveyard path**: Folk narratives have the dying see her coming from the cemetery.
- **Doorway**: Nineteenth-century belief also places her waiting at the door for an infant carried to baptism; at a death-watch the sick may ask that the door be opened for her.

The figure is Lithuanian in attestation (Prussian–Lithuanian written sources and later all-Lithuanian folklore), not a primary Latvian cult name.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

Rites around Giltinė are less a temple cult than death-watch custom and apotropaic trickery:
- **Death omens**: Owl hoot at night; dog barking with no visitor; beams creaking; doors opening alone; threefold knocking at windows or doors.
- **Deceiving death**: Folk tales tell of cutting her long nails or forked tongue to postpone a death.
- **Vėlinės remembrance**: In some localities she was named when flatbreads were baked and given to beggars so they would pray for the departed.
- **Deathbed candles**: Blessed Grabnyčios (Candlemas) candles are lit beside the dying and at funerals as part of the death-watch.
- **Deathbed reception**: When the dying see her, households may open the door so she can take the one whose time has come.
