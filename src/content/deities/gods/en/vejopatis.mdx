## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Vėjopatis (also recorded as *Wejopatis*, *Wejpons*, *Wejdiews* — “Wind Lord” / *Vėjo dievas*) is a western Lithuanian wind god. His name is a transparent compound of Lithuanian *vėjas* (“wind”) and *pats* / *patis* (“lord, master”).

Written attestation begins late and rests on a single primary observer:
- **Matthäus Praetorius (*Deliciae Prussicae*, late 17th century, c. 1690)**: First records Vėjopatis as a wind deity honoured by fishermen. He describes seeing a bark cult figure fastened to a mast among the oars at a fisherman’s place in **Karklė** on the Curonian Lagoon — set up in place of a flag.

Encyclopedias treat him as functionally close to, and sometimes identified with, the Prussian / western Lithuanian sea-storm god *Bangpūtys*, and compare him with the Prussian seafarers’ god *Bardaitis* / *Gardaitis*.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

Vėjopatis governs wind, especially the stormy weather feared and petitioned by lagoon and coastal fishermen. Praetorius notes that he was particularly honoured in rough weather. His sphere overlaps the maritime wind–wave complex of Lithuania Minor rather than the inland sky pantheon alone.

Lithuanian folklore more broadly links personified wind with death and *vėlės*; Razauskas’s study of the wind demon / *vėjukas* complex uses Praetorius’s Vėjopatis image as a historical anchor for that wider wind mythology.

**Relations:**
- **Close counterpart**: *Bangpūtys* — encyclopedias call them related, close in function, or (in some scholarship) partially or fully identical.
- **Prussian parallel**: *Bardaitis* / *Gardaitis* — Praetorius himself guessed the Karklė figure might once have represented Bardaitis; Balsys treats the names as possibly the same coastal wind–sea power under different tribal labels.
- **Latvian functional parallel**: *Vēja māte* (and the older song-world *Vēja dievs* / *Vējš*) — correspondence of wind rule, not identity as one deity.

## 3. Character and Visuals

Praetorius’s Karklė cult image (and the drawing he adds) is the sole detailed iconography:
- **Cult figure**: Bark / wooden human figure fixed to a mast by the oars, used like a weather ensign.
- **Two faces**: One looking forward, one backward (*dviveidis*).
- **Mouths**: Lips blown out / open as if exhaling wind.
- **Wings**: Large wings on the shoulders.
- **Pose**: Right arm raised, left arm lowered.
- **Held attributes**: Small barrel (*statinaitė* / *muciņa*) in the raised right hand; fish in the lowered left hand.
- **Head attribute**: A rooster perched on the head.

VLE notes that two-faced gods are unusual in Lithuanian mythology; Beresnevičius suggested the image may reflect coastal Slavic influence.

## 4. Natural Habitat, Related Locations

Vėjopatis belongs to the Lithuania Minor / Curonian Lagoon fishing world:
- **Karklė (Curonian Lagoon shore)**: Find-spot of the bark cult figure Praetorius saw at a fisherman’s place.
- **Western Lithuanian / Prussian coastal belt**: Shared domain with Bangpūtys and Bardaitis lore among Scalvian–Nadrovian and Lietuvininkai fishermen.
- **Latvian coast (comparative)**: *Vēja māte* holds the parallel wind-and-sail domain in dainas.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

Sparse but concrete coastal practices attach to this wind–sea complex:
- **Storm honour**: Vėjopatis was especially worshipped when the weather was stormy.
- **Weather signs (*vėjones*)**: Praetorius also mentions wind devices that prophesied according to the wind.
- **Related Bardaitis fish rite** (Prussian / Sudovian book tradition, compared to Vėjopatis): Sailors boiled fish, set it on clean straw in the barn, ate and drank from small bowls; a priest (*Segnote* / *Wourskaite*) declared what wind would blow and where to fish.
- **Weather-vane afterlife of the image**: Razauskas links Praetorius’s winged wind figure to later Lithuanian *vėjukas* weather vanes as possible fossilised cult imagery.
