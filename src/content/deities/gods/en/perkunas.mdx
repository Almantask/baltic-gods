## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Perkūnas is the supreme Baltic god of thunder, lightning, justice, and atmospheric order. His name stems from the Proto-Indo-European root *\*per-kw-u-* meaning oak, mountain, or strike (cf. Sanskrit *Parjanya*, Old Norse *Fjörgyn*, Latin *quercus*). Historical chronicles document Perkūnas more frequently than any other Baltic deity. The 1252 Hypatian Codex records King Mindaugas offering sacrifices to *Perkunos*. Peter von Dusburg's 1326 *Chronicon Terrae Prussiae* describes the sacred sanctuary of Romuva, where an eternal oak-fire burned continuously before the triadic image of Perkūnas. Jan Łasicki’s 1582 work details ritual bull sacrifices offered to Perkūnas to ward off drought.

Scholars Marija Gimbutas and Nijolė Laurinkienė emphasize Perkūnas' role as guardian of cosmic order (*dharma*), battling the subterranean chaos embodied by Velnias.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

Perkūnas governs the heavens, storm clouds, fire, and divine retribution. He is envisioned as a mature warrior with a copper beard, riding across the skies in a fiery chariot drawn by winged horses or goats. In his hands he wields thunderbolts (belemnite stones), bronze axes, and hammers used to strike down chthonic demons.

Perkūnas protects humanity from drought and evil spirits. In spring, his first strike unlocks the frozen soil (Žemyna), allowing life to bloom. As god of justice, oaths were sworn beneath sacred oak trees or while touching iron blades, calling upon Perkūnas to strike down perjurers.

## 3. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

The cult of Perkūnas was tied to sacred groves and perpetual flame:

- **The Perpetual Flame (Zničas)**: In sacred groves (*alkas*), priestesses (*vaidilutės*) and priests (*kriviai*) maintained a perpetual oak-wood fire beneath ancient oak trees. Allowing the fire to die out was considered a catastrophic omen.
- **Rain and Drought Supplications**: During severe droughts, communities gathered at sacred oaks to sacrifice a black bull, goat, or rooster, pouring ale libations to invoke refreshing rain.
- **Lightning Protection Rites**: Stone axes (*Perkūno kulkos*) and carved six-petaled rosette symbols (*Perkūno žvaigždė*) were placed on rafters and lintels to protect homesteads from lightning strikes.

## 4. Folklore, Dainas, and Oral Tradition

Baltic folklore features countless tales detailing the eternal duel between Perkūnas and Velnias. Velnias seeks refuge under stones, trees, or cattle, while Perkūnas pursues him with lightning bolts. Consequently, villagers closed windows during thunderstorms, avoided standing beneath solitary oaks, and held iron keys for protection.

Folk songs (*Dainos*) portray him as a stern yet benevolent protector:
> "Perkūnas thundered,  
> The heavens shook,  
> The green oak stood firm,  
> The demon trembled."

## 5. Sacred Geography and Cultural Legacy

Sacred Perkūnas oaks and hill-sanctuaries span the Baltic region. Prominent sites include Rambynas Hill, the sacred groves of Šventoji, Samogitian hillforts, and the Turaida oaks of Latvia. Perkūnas symbols—lightning patterns, oak leaves, and double-axes—remain prominent motifs in Baltic folk art, weaving, and blacksmithing.
