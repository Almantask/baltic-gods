## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Perkūnas is the active Baltic god of thunder, lightning, justice, and atmospheric order (operating in divine hierarchy as the executive deity under the supreme sky sovereign Andajus). His name is usually linked to Proto-Indo-European *\*perkʷu-* (“oak”) and/or *\*per-* (“strike”); VLE notes the etymology is not fully settled (cf. Sanskrit *Parjanya*, Old Norse *Fjörgyn*, Latin *quercus*, and Slavic *Perun* — the last often compared, sometimes phonologically disputed). Historical name variants include Lithuanian *Perkūnas*, Latvian *Pērkons* (or *Pērkonītis*), and Old Prussian *Percunis*; *Parkuns* is attested as Yotvingian. Lithuanian folk epithets include *Dundulis*, *Tarškulis*, *Grumulis*, and *Dievaitis*; Latvian tradition prefers *Debesu kalējs*, *Pērkoņtēvs*, *Vecais tēvs*.

Historical chronicles document Perkūnas more frequently than any other Baltic deity:
- **1252 Hypatian Codex**: Records King Mindaugas offering sacrifices to *Diviriks* (reconstructed by scholars W. Mannhardt, N. Vėlius, and G. Beresnevičius as *Dievų rikis* — "Ruler/Leader of Gods", an epithet of Perkūnas).
- **1261 Volhynian / Malalas Chronicle Gloss**: The legend of Sovijus lists Perkūnas alongside *Teliavelis*, *Andajus*, and *Žvorūna*.
- **13th-Century Livonian Rhymed Chronicle**: Line 1434 records the Baltic god under the name *Perkune* (termed an *apgot* or pagan idol).
- **1326 Peter von Dusburg (*Chronicon Terrae Prussiae*)**: Describes the supreme sanctuary of Romuva in Nadruvia and its *krivis*; the evergreen oak, triadic idols, and perpetual oak-wood fire are first detailed by Simon Grunau (c. 1517–1526), not Dusburg.
- **15th-Century Jan Długosz (*Annales*)**: Compares Perkūnas to Roman Jupiter and notes sacred fire; elaborate Vilnius temple choreography often stems from later writers (Stryjkowski, Rivijus/Narbutt) and is historiographically contested.
- **16th–17th c. drought rites (Łasicki names Percunos; Fabricius/Maleckis detail the rite)**: Łasicki (1582) lists *Percunos* among atmospheric gods; the goat (*ožys*)/black-calf and beer libation for rain is attested in Fabricius (~1611–1620) and related ethnographic notes, not as a Łasicki drought narrative.
- **Simon Grunau / Bretkūnas (Prussian Romuva tradition)**: Describes Perkūnas among a triad in a multi-trunked evergreen oak, with flint/stone attributes and a perpetual flame tended by *vaidelotės*; Maciej Stryjkowski (1582) mainly characterizes weather functions rather than the flint-hammer idol tableau.

Scholars Marija Gimbutas and Nijolė Laurinkienė emphasize Perkūnas' role as guardian of cosmic order (*kosminė tvarka*), constantly defending the mortal realm against subterranean chaos.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

Perkūnas occupies the active middle and upper atmosphere in the Baltic tripartite cosmology (Sky, Earth, Underworld). While Andajus (Dievas) remains the remote, sovereign sky creator (*Deus otiosus*), Perkūnas acts as the celestial judge, ruler of weather, and executor of divine order (*Darna*).

His primary functional domains include:
- **Meteorological & Seasonal Awakening**: Controls storm clouds, lightning, and rain. In spring, his first thunderbolt unlocks the frozen soil (Žemyna), fertilizing the earth for planting.
- **Divine Retribution & Morality**: Sworn oaths were solemnized beneath sacred oaks or while touching iron blades. Perkūnas strikes perjurers, proud tyrants, and evil spirits.
- **Cosmic Dualism**: Represents the primary celestial counterpart in the eternal duel against chthonic Velnias (Velns), who seeks refuge under stones, hollow oaks, or cattle.

## 3. Character and Visuals

Folklore and traditional dainas depict Perkūnas as a mature, stern warrior with a copper or fiery red beard (*ugninė barzda*) and flashing eyes, clad in copper or iron armor:
- **Chariot & Steeds**: Rides across the sky in a fiery chariot drawn by two winged horses or heavy goats (*ožiai*), producing the sound of thunder.
- **Weapons & Tools**: Wields thunderstone projectiles (*Perkūno kulkos* / belemnites), a bronze double-axe, stone hammers, and a bow shooting lightning arrows.
- **Sacred Symbols**: The sacred oak tree (*Quercus robur* / *ąžuolas*), six-petaled rosette (*Perkūno žvaigždė* / solar wheel), double-axe motifs, and iron blades.

## 4. Natural Habitat, Related Locations

Perkūnas' cult was centered on elevated terrain, waterside oak groves, and lightning-struck landmarks across Lithuania, Latvia, and Old Prussia:
- **Sacred Geography**: Mountain tops, hillforts (*piliakalniai*, *pērkonkalni*), sacred groves (*alkai*, *elkai*), and hilltop altars.
- **Romuva Sanctuary (Nadruvia / Old Prussia)**: Central Prussian sanctuary with a multi-trunked evergreen oak housing the sacred fire.
- **Vilnius Šventaragis Valley (Lithuania)**: Historical reported sanctuary where perpetual oak fires were maintained by priests (*kriviai*) and priestesses (*vaidilutės*).
- **Rambynas Hill (Pagėgiai, Lithuania)**: Skalvian hillfort associated with Perkūnas offerings and storm vigils.
- **Šventoji Oak Grove (Lithuania)**: Coastal oak sanctuary associated with northern Perkūnas cult geography (project meta). *Pekas kalns* (Latvia) is a debated hillfort (Beverīna tradition), not a documented weather sanctuary of Pērkons.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

The cult of Perkūnas involved both public sanctuary rites and family protection customs:
- **Perpetual Flame (Zničas)**: Priestesses (*vaidilutės*) maintained an unbroken fire of oak wood beneath sacred oaks. Extinguishing the flame was viewed as a dire community omen.
- **Drought & Rain Supplications**: In times of severe drought, communities gathered at sacred oaks to offer a goat (*ožys*) or bull (*jautis*), pouring ale libations to invoke rain.
- **Seasonal Festivals & Taboos**: First spring thunder unlocks the earth in folklore (tilling often delayed until then). The neotraditional feast *Jorė* is sometimes linked to this motif, though academic sources treat a distinct *Jorė* deity as weakly attested. Thursdays (*Ketvirtadienis*) were dedicated to Perkūnas in Lithuanian tradition (farm-work bans); Latvian folk calendars more often mark *Pēterdiena* (29 June) as *Pērkona diena*.
- **Home Protection Rites**: Stone axes (*Perkūno kulkos*) and carved six-petaled rosettes were placed under rafters to protect homes from lightning. Dried juniper (*kadagys*) was burned on hearth coals during storms.

### ⚠️ Disputed / soft
- **PIE etymology**: Present as debated (*perkʷu-* oak vs *per-* strike), not a single settled “*per-kw-u-* = oak/mountain/strike” — [Baltnexus](https://etimologija.baltnexus.lt/?w=perk%C5%ABnas) — `Ctrl+F: '*perkʷu-'`.
- **Epithet mix**: Dundulis/Tarškulis/Dievaitis = LT (VLE); *Debesu kalējs* = LV; do not list all as pan-Baltic chronicle epithets.
- **Copper-armored warrior / winged horses / six-petaled “Perkūno žvaigždė”**: Folklore + Prussian/romantic synthesis; LV dainas prefer *Vecais tēvs*, black/white horses, *ugunskrusts*.
- **Thursday = Perkūnas day**: Confirmed in LT (VLE — `Ctrl+F: 'ketvirtadienis'`); LV folk calendar points more to **Pēterdiena (29 Jun)** as *Pērkona diena*, not weekly Thursday.
- **Jorė = first-thunder feast**: First-thunder earth-unlock is folk-confirmed; equating *Jorė* with Perkūnas is largely neotraditional.
