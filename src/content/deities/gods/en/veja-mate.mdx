## 1. Etymology & Historical Chronicles

Vēja māte (Latvian: *Vēja māte*; Lithuanian: *Vėjo motina / Vėjo motė / Vėjopatis*; Old Prussian: *Wētis / Weja Maat*) is the goddess and mother of the winds in Baltic mythology. The theonym is an archaic genitive compound uniting Proto-Baltic *\*wējas* (derived from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *\*h₂wē-* / *\*h₂weh₁-*, meaning "to blow", via the participial stem *\*h₂wéh₁-n̥t-s*, "wind") with Proto-Baltic *\*mātē* (from PIE *\*méh₂tēr*, "mother"). The theonym is directly cognate with Sanskrit *vā́ta-* / *vāyú-*, Latin *ventus*, and Gothic *winds*.

In comparative Baltic mythology, Vēja māte represents the sovereign feminine ruler of atmospheric currents within the Latvian nature mothers (*Mātes*) matrix. While Lithuanian and Old Prussian chronicle traditions recorded masculine theonyms such as the wind master **Vėjopatis** (Jan Lasicki, 1582, *De diis Samagitarum*) and the storm blower **Bangpūtys** (Matthäus Praetorius, c. 1690, *Deliciae Prussicae*), Lithuanian fairy tales (ATU 400, 461) and Latvian folklore preserve *Vēja māte / Vėjo motina* as the celestial mother of the winds.

The earliest historical chronicle attestation of Vēja māte appears in the 17th century in the theological treatises of Courland Lutheran superintendent Paul Einhorn (*Reformatio Gentis Letticae*, 1636; *Historia Lettica*, 1649), who catalogued *Weja mahte* (*die Windmutter* / Mother of the Wind) among the principal elemental mothers venerated by Baltic peasants and mariners.

Her living mythological role is extensively documented across hundreds of classical folk songs in Krišjānis Barons' *Latvju dainas* (specifically LD 30890, LD 30910, LD 31003, LD 2867, LD 30730, LD 30825, LD 33983). Her mythological structure and ritual role have been analyzed in foundational monographs by Haralds Biezais (*Die Hauptgöttinnen der alten Letten*, 1955), Pēteris Šmits (*Latviešu mitoloģija*, 1926), Dainius Razauskas (*Vėjūkas*, 2004), and *Latvijas Nacionālā enciklopēdija*.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, & Sacred Domain

In Baltic cosmology, Vēja māte governs atmospheric space, regulating breezes, gales, tempests, and directional winds across land and sea.

### Mother of the Four Wind Sons

* **The Cardinal Winds (*Vēja dēli*)**: In Baltic folklore and fairy tales, Vēja māte is the mother and sovereign commander of the Four Wind Sons representing the cardinal directions:
  * **Ziemeļvējš (North Wind / Lithuanian: *Šiaurys*)**: The biting, icy winter wind bringing blizzards (*putenis*).
  * **Dienvidvējš (South Wind / Lithuanian: *Pietys*)**: The warm, thaw-bringing southern breeze.
  * **Austrumvējš (East Wind / Lithuanian: *Austrenis*)**: The dry, crisp morning wind from the dawn horizon.
  * **Rietumvējš (West Wind / Lithuanian: *Rietenis / Vakarinis*)**: The moist maritime wind blowing from the Baltic Sea that casts amber onto the beach.
* **Wind Daughters (*Vēja meitas*)**: Gentle breeze spirits who assist in weaving light mist veils over morning meadows.

### Maritime Sailing & Partnership with the Sea Mother

* **Co-Ruler of Marine Voyages**: In coastal folklore, mariners pray simultaneously to **Jūras māte** and **Vēja māte**. While the Sea Mother controls the underlying water swell, Vēja māte lifts and fills the white sails (*"cilâ viegļi baltajos zēģelišus"*).

### Agricultural Patroness of Winnowing

* **The Threshing Barn Wind**: In agrarian life, Vēja māte was invoked during grain winnowing (*vētīšana* / Lithuanian: *vėtymas*) in the threshing barn (*rija*), providing the steady draft required to separate grain from chaff without scattering the harvest.

### Dual Elemental Nature

* **Beneficent Guardian**: Gently rocks birds' nests in tree branches, dries soaked fields, and propels ships safely homeward.
* **Fierce Storm Gale**: Bends and snaps heavy timber (*"visus mežus locidama"*), whips up coastal squalls, and overturns vessels if maritime taboos are broken.

## 3. Character & Visuals

Baltic folk poetry depicts Vēja māte as a dynamic, swift, and atmospheric sovereign:

* **Streaming Untamed Hair**: Adorned with long, unbraided hair (*vaļēji mati*) that billows across the sky in all directions like wind-blown clouds.
* **Cloud & Mist Garments**: Dressed in flowing grey and white woolen shawls and cloud mantles (*pelēka sagša, miglas villaine*).
* **Weaver of Sea-Foam**: Mythologically portrayed sitting at a cosmic loom above the waters, weaving sea-foam fabrics with reed combs (*"Niedŗu šķieti, putu audi, Vēja māte audejiņa"*).
* **Treetop Striding**: Strides across the canopies of dry oaks and birch groves, rocking forest branches and whistling through hollow wood.
* **Breath of Squalls**: Exhales gusts of wind from puffed cheeks, driving rain and snow clouds across the horizon.

## 4. Natural Habitat & Related Locations

The sacred geography of Vēja māte encompasses high exposed elevations, coastal promontories, and dynamic wind barriers:

* **Cape Kolka (*Kolkasrags*)**: The prominent promontory where the open Baltic Sea (*Dižjūra*) and the Gulf of Riga (*Mazjūra*) collide—the supreme geographical sanctuary where the cross-winds of Vēja māte and the waves of Jūras māte meet in perpetual turbulence.
* **Highland Summits & Hilltops**: High windy ridges such as **Gaiziņkalns** (the highest peak in Latvia) and sacred Lithuanian *alkakalniai* (*Šatrija*, *Rambynas*).
* **Coastal Sand Dunes**: The shifting sand barriers of the **Curonian Spit (*Kuršu kāpas / Kuršių nerija*)** and the Baltic coastline of Kurzeme and Vidzeme.
* **Forest Canopies (*Mežu galotnes*)**: Elevated woodland groves where the rushing roar of the wind was recognized as her living presence.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, & Folk Practices

Ethnographic records document precise customs observed by farmers, millers, and seafarers to secure Vēja māte's favor:

* **Winnowing Offerings (*Vētīšanas Ziedojumi*)**: Farmers tossed a handful of meal, flour, or grain into the breeze (*"sauja miltų vėjui"*) to compensate Vēja māte for separating chaff from seed.
* **"Buying the Wind" (*Pirkt Vēju*)**: Coastal sailors cast silver coins, breadcrumbs, salt, or tobacco into oncoming wind gusts or breaking surf to purchase favorable sailing conditions.
* **Whistling Taboo (*Svilpošanas Aizliegums*)**: Strict prohibitions forbade whistling indoors, near domestic hearths, or aboard boats, as whistling mimicked wind demons and provoked destructive storms. Controlled, low whistling was strictly restricted to winnowers calling a gentle draft.
* **Storm Pacification Rites**: During violent gales, householders cast crumbs of blessed bread, salt, or hearth ashes toward the approaching squall to deflect the wind's fury.
