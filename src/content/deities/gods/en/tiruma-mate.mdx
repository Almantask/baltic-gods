## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Tīruma māte (Latvian *Tīruma māte / Lauka māte / Lauku māte*, "Mother of the Plowed Field"; Lithuanian *Lauko motė / Dirvonų motina*; closely associated with *Zemes māte / Jumis*) is the Latvian agrarian deity presiding over arable cultivated soil, plowing furrows, seed germination, and cereal harvests. The teonym combines *tīrums* ("cultivated field, arable land" < Proto-Baltic *\*tīr-*, from *\*tīrs* "clean, cleared, cultivated") with *māte* ("mother" < Proto-Indo-European *\*méh₂tēr*).

Historical chronicles and comparative folkloric research record the distinct agrarian veneration of the arable field mother:

* **Paul Einhorn (*Historia Lettica*, 1649)**: Documented *Lauku māte* (Mother of Fields) as the divinity presiding over crops, seedbeds, and agricultural fieldwork, receiving libations and food offerings before spring sowing.
* **Krišjānis Barons (*Latvju dainas*, 1894–1915)**: The Latvian folk song corpus preserves numerous field-blessing dainas celebrating Tīruma māte walking the dark furrows to nurture grain shoots (*Tīruma mātīte, zelta kurpītēm, Staigā pa manu rudzu tīrumiņu...* LD 7729-0).
* **Comparative Academic Analysis**: Haralds Biezais (*Die himmlische Götterfamilie der alten Letten*, 1972), Pēteris Šmits (*Latviešu mitoloģija*, 1926), Janīna Kursīte (*Mītiskais folklorā*, 1999), and the *Latvijas Nacionālā enciklopēdija* establish that Tīruma māte is the specialized agricultural hypostasis of the human-worked soil, clearly differentiated from the wild, unplowed cosmic earth (*Zemes māte*).

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

In Baltic agricultural cosmology, uncultivated earth required human labor and divine sanctification to become fertile cereal-bearing land.

### Sovereign of the Furrow and Cereal Vitality

* **Furrow Fertility (*Vagas svētība*)**: Blesses the freshly turned earth opened by the plow, infusing the dark soil with regenerative moisture and vitality.
* **Grain Seed Germination**: Protects sown rye, barley, wheat, and oat grains from rot, frost, and drought during early spring sprouting.
* **Cooperation with Jumis**: Operates alongside the double-grain fertility spirit Jumis, ensuring that cereal stalks grow tall, double-eared, and laden with grain.
* **Harvest Sheaf Guardian**: Oversees the cutting of the final grain sheaf (*nuba / pēdējais kūlītis*), preserving the vegetative essence of the field for next year's sowing.

## 3. Character and Visuals

Latvian dainas portray Tīruma māte through golden agrarian and earthy attire:

* **Golden-Shoed Field Matron**: Depicted as a tall, graceful woman wearing golden shoes (*zelta kurpītes*) and an amber-embroidered apron walking gently over the furrows so as not to crush the tender rye shoots.
* **Sheaf of Ripe Cereal**: Carries a bundle of golden rye ears (*rudzu vārpas*) and a wooden seed-sowing basket (*sētuve*).
* **Crown of Cereal Blossoms**: Adorned with a wreath woven from rye, barley, and blue cornflowers (*rudzupuķes*).
* **Dark Soil Mantle**: Draped in an earth-colored woolen mantle symbolizing the rich humus of well-tilled soil.

## 4. Natural Habitat and Related Locations

The sacred space of Tīruma māte encompasses the agricultural fields across the Baltic landscape:

* **Plowed Grain Fields (*Aramzeme / Rudzu lauks*)**: The vast cereal plains of Zemgale, Vidzeme, Kurzeme, and Latgale.
* **Furrow Boundary Balks (*Lauka eža / Vagas*)**: The grassy ridges between adjoining fields where stones were gathered and offerings deposited.
* **Threshing Barns and Grain Storage (*Rija / Klēts*)**: The farmstead structures where harvested crops were dried and stored.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Folk Practices

Baltic agrarian tradition observed rigorous rituals to honor Tīruma māte throughout the farming year:

* **First Furrow Offering (*Pirmās vagas auka*)**: When the plow opened the first furrow in spring, the plowman placed a slice of rye bread, a hard-boiled egg, or poured beer into the overturned soil.
* **Barefoot Sowing Rite**: Sowers walked the plowed field barefoot to maintain direct sacred contact with the soil while praying for Tīruma māte's blessing.
* **Jumis Catching at Harvest**: During the final reaping, reapers wove the last ears into a symbolic crown and tied the "beard" of the field (*Juma bārda*), leaving a small tuft uncut as an offering.
* **Taboo on Cursing the Soil**: Strict prohibitions forbade spitting upon the plowed ground, swearing in the field, or striking the earth with iron tools on Sundays.
