## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Jumis is the Latvian field-fertility spirit and grain deity whose visible sign is any doubled growth—two ears on one stalk (*dubultvārpa*), twin nuts or apples, or an egg with two yolks. The name’s stem *jum-* belongs with Indo-European vocabulary of joining / pairing / binding (cf. Sanskrit *yamá*, Avestan *yǝ̄ma* as linguistic twin-cognates of the *yem-/*jum-* set); folk and scholarly usage treat *jumis* first as the double plant itself and then as the personified growth-force that dwells in it.

The word enters written Latvian sources in early modern lexica: L. Depkin’s early-18th-century Riga dictionary first records *jumis*, and later 18th-century dictionaries by J. Lange and G. F. Stender gloss it as two nuts, apples, or ears grown together. The living cult and *dainas* preserve a far older agricultural ritual complex around catching and housing that double growth.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

Jumis governs crop increase and granary fullness. Each grain (and flax) may have its own Jumis—rye Jumis, barley Jumis, flax Jumis—and the farmer whose field still holds Jumis is thought to bake strong bread. In summer he stays in the field (often under a grey stone or at the field’s edge); at harvest he is sought in the last uncut patch or last sheaf; through winter he is kept in the barn, bin, or place of honour so fertility returns with the next sowing.

**Relations:** Jumja māte / Jumaliņa (female counterpart) and Jumalēni (children) in some song traditions; horses and a cart that haul the harvest home; typological last-sheaf parallels with Lithuanian Jievaras and Prussian Curcho / Kurka field-driving rites; wealth-attracting *pūķis* said in some beliefs to lie beside a stored Jumis ear in the granary.

## 3. Character and Visuals

Folklore paints Jumis as a tiny, aged little man dressed from the harvest:
- **Looks**: Span-tall (*sprīdi garš*); old; bearded; green and yellow colouring.
- **Clothing**: Garments of plants—barley skirts, rye shirt, hop-blossom cap (*apinīša cepurīte*); yellow boots; green-red vest in song variants; grain wreath (*vainadziņš*) on the head in harvest songs.
- **Accessories / tools**: Golden broom (*zalta slūta*) to sweep fallen ears into the stack.
- **Vehicles**: Horses and cart for bringing grain from the drying-barn to the granary.
- **Symbols**: The Jumis sign (*Jumja zīme*)—a forked double-ear / W-shaped ornament—carved or hung on barn and house roof ends; the living double ear or twin fruit itself.

## 4. Natural Habitat, Related Locations

His domain is the cultivated field and the farmstead stores that keep seed and bread through winter:
- **Field**: Last standing rye, barley, or flax patch; stone heaps and the grey field-stone under which songs say he sleeps.
- **Farmstead**: Granary (*klēts*), drying-barn (*rija*), and the indoor place of honour where the caught Jumis wreath or ear is hung.
- **Landscape of songs**: Strongest in Zemgale and central Vidzeme harvest traditions; the graphic Jumis sign is widespread on Latvian wooden roof ridges and textile ornament.

## 5. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

Harvest-end rites secure Jumis for the coming year:
- **Catching Jumis (*Jumja ņemšana* / *Jumja ķeršana*)**: Reapers close on the last patch; the last sheaf or a knotted artificial jumis is bound, sometimes pressed to the earth under a stone so he winters in the field, then carried home in procession.
- **Housing the double ear**: Natural or braided Jumis ears and grain crowns are hung in the granary, barn end, or room of honour (and sometimes in a girl’s dowry chest) until the next sowing.
- **Apjumības / Miķeļi**: Autumn harvest-closing feast honouring Jumis with bread (often from three mornings’ milling), beer, and table offerings—crumbs cast under the table for Jumis.
- **Roof and textile signs**: *Jumja zīme* set on granary and house gables as a blessing of increase.

*Dainas* invite him indoors:
> “Nāc, Jumīti, mūs’ mājās,  
> Še būs laba dzīvošana:  
> Došu maizi, došu zirņus,  
> Saldu alu nodzerties.”
