## 1. Etymology and Historical Chronicles

Jumis is the Baltic god of the harvest, double ears of grain, twin fruits, and crop abundance. His name descends from Proto-Baltic *\*jum-* (twin, double join; cf. Sanskrit *Yama*, Avestan *Yima*). Historical records mention Jumis in Dionysius Fabricius' 1610 Livonian chronicle and 17th-century parish records describing harvest rituals.

Mythological research by Haralds Biezais highlights Jumis as the personification of double growth: a double ear of wheat or rye, twin apples, or double egg yolks were revered as physical manifestations of Jumis.

## 2. Cosmology, Functions, and Sacred Domain

Jumis governs grain fields (especially rye and barley), twin growths, harvest festivals (*Jumja diena*), and granary abundance. He is portrayed as a small man clad in grain sheaves or represented symbolically as crossed horse heads (*Jumja zīme*).

Jumis retreats into the last standing stalk of grain during harvest. Cutting the final sheaf required the "Catching of Jumis" ceremony to prevent agricultural fertility from escaping the farmstead.

## 3. Sacred Rites, Offerings, and Cult Rites

Harvest-ending rituals centered around securing Jumis:

- **Catching Jumis (Jumja ņemšana)**: The last standing stalks were bound into a sacred crown or double-roofed sheaf and carried home in triumph to hang in the place of honor.
- **Crossed Horse-Head Roof Gables**: Crossed horse-head carvings (*Jumja zīme*) were mounted on roof gables of barns and houses as protective charms for ongoing wealth.

## 4. Folklore, Dainas, and Oral Tradition

Latvian *Dainas* celebrate Jumis bringing wealth to the granary:
> "Come, Jumis, come, Jumis,  
> Into our own homestead;  
> There is space for you  
> Right in the middle of the barn."

## 5. Sacred Geography and Cultural Legacy

Jumis roof-gable motifs remain iconic architectural symbols throughout Baltic wooden architecture.
