## 1. Name and Appearance

*Sumpurnis* (plural *sumpurņi*; also *suņpurnis*; dialect *sumpuris*) literally points to a dog's snout (*suņa purns*). Female forms include *sumpurniene* / *sumpurnene*. Latvian belief and encyclopedia accounts describe an evil humanoid with a human torso and a dog's head—sometimes a bird's head, a single eye, or a single leg—taller than a person, fur-clad, and dressed in tree leaves. Closest kin are said to be dogs; nobles and kings are marked by especially long tails. Bird-headed kin are less fierce than true dog-heads; females are sometimes described as beautiful. Early lexica gloss the word as a fabulous man-eater (*Hundsschnauze*), and thin dainas still use *sumpurni!* as an insult for a cruel person (e.g. BW / LD trails in ME).

## 2. Man-Eaters of the Deep Forest

When enraged, sumpurņi attack humans and animals, tear them apart, eat the flesh, and drink the blood. Captives not eaten at once may be bound and fattened. The tale *Cilvēku bērni pie sumpurņiem* (ATU 327A; Vecpiebalga) tells of a girl fed nuts and sweet milk until her captors pierce her forehead and lick the blood “like a bear licking honey”; she escapes by shoving the *sumpurniene* into an oven lit for three days and fleeing in ash-filled shoes worn backwards (*purnu papēdī*) so the dog-scent trail is lost—warned not to fall back into their claws. In some chase endings the pursuers try to drink a river dry and burst.

A related cycle (≈ ATU 480 / 327) sends a stepdaughter to a chicken-legged *sumpurņa pils* for fire: she may be made to dance with rattling buttons while the monster sleeps; a helpful mouse aids escape with wood and a purse; the cruel sister who refuses courtesy is locked in, burned on an iron grate, and—in one variant—*velns* hauls the witch’s soul to hell’s cauldron. Folklore contrasts sumpurņi with settled farmers: wanderers and hunters who speak an unintelligible tongue (or bark) and eat human flesh.

Belief texts from Cesvaine, Gulbene, and elsewhere describe a distant “land of dogsnouts,” where they sleep hog-fashion (head to foot), “know no God,” and share the land with one-eyed / one-limbed neighbours who, paired, run like the wind. Šmits flagged elaborate island-kingdom variants (e.g. Priedes Jēkabs of Dole—tribal wars, sliding corpses into an abyss, ship raids for daily human flesh) as *Thousand and One Nights* imports, not native Latvian *teikas*.

## 3. Werewolf Overlap, Belt Curse, and Lower Spirits

Sumpurņi are sometimes confused with *vilkači* (werewolves) and sit with them, *vadātājs*, and similar figures among “lower-level beings” (*zemākā līmeņa būtnes*). Sorcerers could transform others into dogsnouts by girding them with a belt (attested e.g. around Bauska); the enchantment ends only when the belt is untied or torn apart. Lithuanian has lexical cognates (*šumburnis* / *šunburnis* / *šuniaburnis*); LKŽ also records a thin folk sense of *šunburniai* as dog-mouthed man-eaters living elsewhere who slaughter a living person and eat him raw—far thinner than the Latvian tale tradition, and often pejorative. The closer LT shifter parallel remains *vilktakis*.

## 4. Kurbads and Cultural Reception

In the long *Ķēves dēls Kurbads* cycle recorded by Ansis Lerhis-Puškaitis around Džūkste–Pienava, Kurbads fights a Sumpurnis who owns forest meadows and keeps a fierce guardian bird. The monster sucks out a herdsman’s eyes for illicit grazing and keeps a healing-herb jar; the hero seizes him by the dog throat, pins him, and thrice clubs him dead—then shields the bird’s nestlings from the hail the dying Sumpurnis still summons. Later in the same cycle, a multi-headed devil’s wife takes snake and sumpurnis form to avenge her husband.

Rihards Zariņš etched the duel as *Kurbads un sumpurnis* (1908–1911); Rihards Maurs cast a fountain group with Sumpurnis, Bārenīte, and Mātesmeita in Riga’s Kronvalda Park (1933). The Latvian Art Academy carnival has twice taken the title *Sumpurņa pils* (1929; revived 2019). Modern oak woodcarvers also carve fairy-tale *sumpurņi* among other folklore figures (literary craft motif, not cult idols).
